How Much Hotter Could Los Angeles Get?

Yearly temperature change relative to the 1981–2010 historical baseline · CESM2 climate model · CMIP6

Data: CMIP6 CESM2 monthly temperature averaged to yearly values · 5-year centered moving average applied to show trends · Anomaly = yearly temperature − 1981–2010 average

Rationale for Design Decisions

Visual encoding: We use a multi-line time-series chart because position along a common scale (the y-axis) is the most accurate perceptual channel for comparing continuous quantities. Showing all four scenarios on the same axes lets viewers directly read the gap between emission pathways for any given year.

Anomaly vs. raw temperature: We plot temperature change relative to the 1981–2010 baseline rather than raw values. Anomalies are more interpretable because viewers can directly see how much warmer Los Angeles may become compared to recent historical conditions. For example, under the high-emissions scenario, temperatures approach roughly +9°F above the baseline by 2100, while the gap between low- and high-emissions pathways is about 5°F.

Color palette: Gray is used for the historical series to separate past observations from future projections. Green, orange, and crimson represent low-, medium-, and high-emissions scenarios respectively, creating an intuitive progression between pathways while making the scenarios easy to distinguish visually.

Interactions chosen:

  • Scenario toggle buttons: let users isolate any combination of scenarios and directly compare them.
  • Hover tooltip: shows the exact year, temperature anomaly, and raw temperature for every visible scenario so values don't need to be read off the axis.
  • Brush / zoom context chart: lets users drag-select any time window (e.g., 2015–2100 only) to zoom into the main chart, reducing clutter from the 1850–2014 historical baseline.
  • °F / °C toggle: makes the visualization accessible to both US and international audiences without re-loading data.
  • Dynamic end-century annotation: automatically calculates the temperature gap between the highest and lowest visible scenarios in the final decade (2091–2100) to provide instant, comparative context.

Alternatives considered: We considered a ribbon/fan chart showing model uncertainty, but with only one CESM2 member per scenario the single-line approach is more honest. We also considered a heatmap by decade but found it harder to read trends than the line chart. We also chose to apply a 5-year smoothing to the data to highlight the climatic trend over yearly weather noise.

Development Process

Motivation: This visualization is motivated by the need to show how individual choices regarding global emissions directly impact local temperature changes in our community.

Team members: Harshatha Prasanna, Jazely Tong, Aryan Nath

Work split:

  • Data extraction & Python EDA (checkpoint notebook): 3 members (~6 person-hours)
  • D3 main chart, axes, and line rendering: 1 member (~4 hours)
  • Tooltip and scenario buttons: 1 member (~3 hours)
  • Brush/context chart, unit toggle, styling, and writeup: 2 members (~3 hours)

Total: approximately 16 person-hours. The most time-consuming part was wrangling D3's brush + zoom interaction to correctly re-scale the x-axis of the main chart.
